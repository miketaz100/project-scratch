## 0. The assumption every design shares

Every architecture so far (float-arm, crown, porcupine) runs a **pattern engine whose numbers we made up**. Scratch-model §4.4 draws stroke length, frequency, force and phase from `U()` and `N()`, independently, cycle by cycle. These distributions come from observation and judgment, not measurement. The model says so itself: the ethogram proportions are UNKNOWN (§4.1), "the human reference does not exist" (hybrid §2.1), and §9.13 calls instrumenting a human "the highest-value experiment in the program". Three further assumptions are hidden inside that one:

1. **Generative.** A scratch is i.i.d. jitter around a mean. Human motor variability is not white. Errors in reproduced intervals and distances fluctuate as 1/f noise ([Gilden et al. 1995, *Science*](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.7892611)), and the variables are coupled (force dips at reversals, speed with stroke length, finger landing spread with hand speed).
2. **Population-average.** One spec fits Michael. ASMR and hair-play responses are idiosyncratic (prior-art §3.5), and the program cannot even agree on the force window: scratch-model §3.2 gives 0.15–0.3 N per nail, while tip-interface and red team 1 demand 0.3–0.9 N. That is a factor of two to three on the rank-3 variable.
3. **Open-loop.** The device never learns whether a stroke worked. A human scratcher closes that loop all the time: they see the lean-in, hear the sigh and stay on the spot.

This document attacks all three: **measure a real scratcher, replay the measurement, and let Michael's responses choose what plays.** The verdict is in §4. In short, replay is certainly a leap in development speed. It is a leap in sensation only under a condition that one zero-dollar experiment can check, and the closed loop is the more likely sensation win.

## 1. The groundwork: what replay must reproduce, and how many DOF that takes

To work out the fidelity replay needs, I split a recorded four-finger scratch into a hand-level rigid motion on the scalp surface plus small per-finger residuals. Then I asked which channels sit above perceptual tolerance.

| Channel | Human range (scratch-model) | Perceptual tolerance | Replay requirement |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hand path on scalp surface (2 DOF) | rake 15–40 mm, sweep 60–150 mm, 2–20 cm/s, 1–4 Hz | velocity Weber fraction **0.20–0.25** on hairy forearm `[KNOWN]` ([Essick et al. 1988](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3242342)); location: two-point 15–40 mm, so localisation is ~10 mm `[EST]` | ±5 mm, ±15 % speed, bandwidth to ~12 Hz (4 Hz × 3rd harmonic) |
| Hand yaw (stroke direction vs. hair lie) (1 DOF) | wander ±15–30° per few cycles (§4.2) | `[EST]` ±10° | ±10° |
| Per-finger normal force (4 DOF) | 0.05–0.6 N, ±30–50 % stroke to stroke, landing ramps 0.2–0.5 s | force Weber ~0.15–0.2 `[EST]` | ±0.05 N, ≥ 15 Hz |
| Per-finger contact timing (inside the force channel) | landing spread 20–80 ms (§4.2) | tactile temporal-order threshold ~20–40 ms `[EST]` | ±10 ms |
| Per-finger tangential residual (8 DOF) | `[EST]` < 5 mm in P1/P2; 5–15 mm in P4 "spider" | below localisation except in P4 | drop; emulate P4 with force oscillation (test) |
| Nail attack angle (4) | ±2–4° drift (red team 1 §3) | negligible | fixed 45° |
| Lift / region change | ≥ 15 mm clearance, 3–10 cm jumps | — | hand-level |

**Result: a recording collapses to about 7 continuous DOF: 3 on the surface (x, y, yaw) and 4 per-finger force channels at ≥ 15 Hz.** The two candidate machines compare as follows:

- **Float-arm SP1:** 1 DOF (stroke), plus a yaw add-on, with constant dead-weight force.
- **Porcupine:** 2 continuous shell DOF plus a slow discrete selector, with passive constant-force pins (k ≤ 0.02 N/mm, pin-unit §0).

Neither can replay per-finger force envelopes or landing asynchrony. Red team 1's yaw-stagger trick gives a passive landing spread, but it cannot reproduce a recorded one.

The data rate is trivial: 7 channels × 200 Hz × 4 B ≈ 5.6 kB/s, about 20 MB per hour on a microSD card `[EST]`.

## 2. Five candidate leaps

### D1 — The player piano: the stimulus is data, not a model

**(a) Assumption broken:** the scratch pattern must be generated by a parametric engine whose distributions we design.

**(b) Principle.** Capture a human scratch as a "scratch roll" (a time series of hand pose on the head surface plus per-finger force), retarget it to the device's DOF and play it back. There are three tiers:

- **Literal replay:** play the stored segments.
- **Library replay:** cut the recordings at lift-offs into primitive segments and concatenate them in a new order, so the same 40 minutes of recording never repeats as a whole.
- **Learned replay:** fit a small generative model (a Markov chain over primitives plus AR(p) on stroke parameters) to the data, so the statistics are human but the sequence is new.

```
  HUMAN SESSION                         DEVICE
  nail IMU/FSR ─┐                       ┌─ carrier x,y,yaw (servo IK)
  head load cells├─► sync ─► reconstruct ─► scratch roll ─► retarget ─► safety clamp ─┤
  240 fps video ─┘   (tap)   (head frame)   (7 ch @200 Hz)  (+lag comp)  (F≤0.6 N,     └─ 4× finger force
                                             │                            v≤20 cm/s,
                                             └─► segment at lift-offs ─► library ─► player (D3 chooses)
```

**(c) Why it could step-change the sensation.** It attacks rank 1 in §7 (pattern irregularity, priority 25, the most uncertain high-importance variable) and rank 6 (asynchrony). §4.3 says the "machine" percept arrives in 10–30 s because predictable touch is centrally attenuated. An i.i.d. jitter engine is unpredictable stroke by stroke, but its statistics are stationary, flat-spectrum and uncoupled. A human's are correlated (1/f), coupled across variables and phrase-structured. If the brain's "this is a machine" detector keys on that structure, then no amount of tuning `U()` ranges will fix it, and replay will.

How much it could move: the same §7 variable that red team 1 estimates turns "a machine on one spot" from P ≈ 0.4 at 30 s to 0.8 at 2 min. `[UNKNOWN]`

**(d) Plausibility.** The storage, sync and retargeting are routine motion-capture practice. Aescape already captures massage therapists' hand motion and force with sensor gloves and translates it into robot arm programs ([ASME](https://www.asme.org/topics-resources/content/ai-and-robotic-massage,-delivered-on-demand)). A 2026 CMU bathing study captures and transfers contact-rich human-on-human demonstrations, with force, to a soft robot hand ([arXiv 2608.09127](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.09127)).

Robots can deliver pleasant stroking comparable to (slightly below) a human ([Sensors 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9919452/)). A servo tracking a 4 Hz rake with harmonics to 12 Hz lags 20–60 ms. That lag is fixed by identifying the servo once and time-advancing the command, a standard feed-forward. The velocity tolerance (±20 %) is loose enough for STS3215-class servos `[EST]`.

**(e) Cheapest experiment ($0, one evening): the human-hand 2×2.** The same helper scratches Michael in four blind-ordered 60 s blocks:

| | real fingernails | TM1 tip on the Day-0 wand |
|---|---|---|
| **free (human pattern)** | HN | HW |
| **scripted pattern** | SN | SW |

In the "scripted" rows, the helper follows an earbud cue track rendered from the §4.4 engine (click = stroke onset, pitch = force cue, tone = region change), so a human hand executes the machine's pattern. Michael rates realism and "alive-ness" on the test-protocols §O form.

- HN − SN isolates **pattern structure** from contact physics.
- HN − HW isolates **tip physics** from pattern.

If HN ≈ SN, the §4.4 model is already good enough and replay is only a development-speed tool. If HN ≫ SN, human structure matters and D1 is a sensation leap. With a recorder running (§3), the same session also yields the first scratch rolls.

**(f) Replaces / combines:** it replaces the generator inside the pattern engine and keeps everything else, including hold-to-run, the e-stop and the caps. It works on any hardware, but fidelity scales with DOF (§1 and D4).

**(g) Why it might not work.**

1. The 2×2 shows HN ≈ SN.
2. **Replay is open-loop by construction.** The recorded scratcher was responding to Michael's reactions at the time, and replaying those responses without their causes mistimes them. D3 and D5 exist to fix this.
3. A finite library is learnable over weeks.
4. A 1–2 DOF device may project away exactly the structure that mattered.

### D2 — The instrumented head: measure before you design

**(a) Assumption broken:** design numbers come from the literature and estimates (forearm, self-scratch, tablet), not from a person scratching Michael's own hair-covered head.

**(b) Principle.** Michael lies face-down on a massage-style face cradle that sits on a plate over **four load cells**. The best scratcher he knows scratches him with:

- dot-marked nails filmed by a phone at **240 fps**, plus a second phone or webcam for stereo;
- a **6-axis IMU ring** on each finger's middle phalanx (contact detection from > 70 Hz vibration, the same cue the scratch-sensing ring uses ([Padmanabha et al. 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43856-023-00345-2)));
- optionally, **FSR 400s under press-on nails** for per-finger force ratios.

Michael holds a rating knob and a "yes, more of that" button. A cosmetology mannequin head with human hair on the same plate serves for calibration and repeatable tests.

```
        phone 240 fps (+ webcam)                    helper's hand
              \                                 IMU rings ●●●●  (per finger)
               \                                 FSR under press-on nails
                ▼                                      │
        ┌──────────── head (face-down) ───────────┐   ▼
        │  ~4.5 kg preload; scratch adds 0.2–2 N  │  ◄── knob + button in Michael's hand
        └──────────────┬──────────────┬───────────┘
              plate ═══╪══════════════╪═══ plate
                LC1  LC2   ...   LC3  LC4   (10 kg bar cells → 4× HX711 @ 80 SPS)
                       ESP32-S3 + microSD, one timebase
```

**(c) Sensation link.** None directly. D2 is the gate that tells every other design which values to aim at. It turns §3 rows 3.2, 3.5–3.10, 3.13, 3.14 and the §4 ethogram proportions from ESTIMATED into MEASURED-for-Michael. It settles the 0.15–0.3 N vs 0.3–0.9 N conflict in one hour. It answers §9 questions 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 9, 10, 11, 12 and 13 outright, and 8 partially (§3.3 below).

**(d) Plausibility, with numbers.**

- **Load cells.** HX711 on 10 kg cells resolves about 1 g ≈ 0.01 N `[EST]`. Breathing and head shift drift the head-weight preload by ±0.5 N at < 0.5 Hz, so I high-pass at 0.5 Hz. The 1–4 Hz scratch force envelope survives, and the slow drift becomes the lean-in signal (D3).
- **Per-finger split.** Total force plus centre of pressure gives three equations. The four nails are nearly collinear, though, so only the sum and the moment along the finger line are recoverable, which is two equations for four unknowns. The per-finger split therefore needs the FSR ratios, rescaled so that they sum to the load-cell total. FSR 400s activate at about 0.2 N and cost about $3 ([Interlink](https://buyinterlinkelectronics.com/products/fsr-400-short)). They work as ratio and contact sensors, not as absolute gauges.
- **Camera tracking.** MediaPipe-class tracking averages about 3 mm error ([Sensors 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13306769/)), below scalp localisation. The nail tip is reconstructed as the dot position plus a finger-model offset, because tips vanish into the hair.
- **Head frame.** Three markers on a thin headband, plus a free phone photogrammetry scan of Michael's head fitted to an ellipsoid, map nail tips to (θ, φ), region and hair-lie direction.

**(e) Cheapest experiment:** the session itself (§3.3), about $120–140 of sensors and one afternoon.

**(f) Replaces / combines:** it becomes Stage 0b of whatever is built next, beside the Day-0 wand. The wand and D1's 2×2 run in the same session.

**(g) Why it might not work.**

- A face-down cradle and a self-conscious helper may not produce "natural" scratching. Mitigation: record 30+ minutes, and discard the first 10.
- Press-on nails change the edge. Mitigation: run FSR blocks and bare-nail blocks separately.
- Load-cell drift from head motion may swamp low-force P4 episodes (0.05–0.2 N) `[UNKNOWN]`.

### D3 — Closed loop: the device searches its own space and converges on Michael

**(a) Assumption broken:** the right pattern is a fixed target, designed offline for an average head.

**(b) Principle.** The search runs on two layers.

- **Layer 1, a restless bandit over the replay library.** The arms are 10–20 clusters of recorded segments (rakes against the lie at the occiput, slow nape traces, crown spiders, and so on). Discounted Thompson sampling plays more of what earns approval. The discount models satiety: liking and wanting of repeated strokes decline over repetitions ([touch satiety, PMC4236195](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4236195/)), so an arm's value decays while it is played and recovers while it rests.
- **Layer 2, preferential Bayesian optimisation** over 4 continuous transforms applied to whatever plays: force scale (0.5–1.5×), speed scale (0.7–1.3×), asynchrony scale (0–2×) and dwell (5–20 s). It learns from occasional A/B pairs: "this or the last one?" Preference-based Bayesian optimisation has been used to tune the perceived realism of haptic rendering ([ResearchGate 370001590](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/370001590_Preference-Based_Human-in-the-Loop_Optimization_for_Perceived_Realism_of_Haptic_Rendering)). In wearable robotics, Bayesian optimisation found the optimal two-parameter hip-exosuit assistance in **21.4 ± 1.0 min** ([Ding et al. 2018, *Sci. Robotics*](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/scirobotics.aar5438)), and CMA-ES did the same for a four-parameter ankle exoskeleton ([Zhang et al. 2017, *Science*](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aal5054)).

```
   library arms ──► bandit picks segment ──► PBO transform (F×, v×, async×, dwell) ──► device
        ▲                                                                              │
        └──── reward: button "yes" (sparse) · A/B choice (rare) · lean-in Δload · EDA phasic ◄┘
```

**Reward signals, ranked honestly:**

| Signal | What it measures | Usable as | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Yes, more" button / A/B choice | valence, explicit | primary reward | best SNR; risk: judging breaks the trance |
| Lean-in (cradle or float load rises 0.1–0.5 N in 1–3 s) | approach behaviour | implicit reward | promising `[UNKNOWN]`; **needs a grounded device** (impossible on a helmet that moves with the head) |
| Skin conductance (Grove GSR) | arousal, not valence: ASMR *raises* SCL ([Poerio 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0196645)), stroking sometimes lowers it | phasic "tingle event" flag only | sign-ambiguous; not a reward |
| HRV (Polar H10 RR) | parasympathetic shift; HRV rises with CT-velocity stroking ([PubMed 28723347](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28723347/)) | session-level comparison (≥ 60 s windows) | too slow per stroke; slow stroking and rhythmic touch gave *similar* autonomic responses despite different pleasantness ([Biol. Psychol. 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0301051124002175)) |
| Neck EMG (MyoWare) | relaxation / shiver | secondary | noisy; low value |

**(c) Sensation link.** §7 ranks 3, 4, 6, 9 and 14 get set for Michael's head and hair instead of the literature's forearm. The bandit's exploration is principled irregularity concentrated near what he likes, which directly addresses the §4.3 habituation horizon. It is also the closest machine analogue to what a human scratcher actually does: stay where the reaction is good, and move on when it fades. Expected effect: personalisation alone could plausibly be worth 1–2 points on a 10-point realism/pleasure scale, simply by correcting a factor-of-2 force error and a speed-band choice (3 vs 12 cm/s, §9 Q2) `[EST]`.

**(d) Plausibility.** With 4 dimensions and about 35 s per pairwise trial, roughly 40–80 comparisons take 25–45 minutes, spread over 2–3 sessions `[EST]`. Button-only bandit updates cost no attention. Compute is trivial, either on the laptop or on the controller.

**(e) Cheapest experiment.** Wizard-of-Oz on the existing SP1: three parameters (force via the float's dead weight is not live, so use speed, stroke length and dwell) and one extra $3 button. Over two evenings, compare a fixed best-guess pattern against the optimiser's converged pattern, blind, on day 3. Before any motor exists: the helper scratches while a laptop bandit tells them, through an earbud, which primitive to do next, and Michael presses "yes".

**(f) Replaces / combines:** it sits on top of D1's library, or on top of the §4.4 engine if D1 fails. It works with any hardware. A grounded rig gains the lean-in channel.

**(g) Why it might not work.**

- The act of judging suppresses the relaxed, ASMR-like state.
- Preferences are noisy and drift within a session.
- Optimisers chase intensity ("more force"), which feels best at minute 1 and worst at minute 15.

Mitigation: optimise on session-final ratings plus the button, and keep the force cap mechanical.

### D4 — The replay hand: per-finger force becomes a primary channel

**(a) Assumption broken:** force per contact is a passive constant (dead weight, long soft spring), and only the group moves. This is the porcupine's decoupling win (force set by the spring), and replay makes it a liability.

**(b) Principle.** The §1 DOF budget, built as hardware: a 3-DOF surface carrier (x, y, yaw on the skull curvature) carrying **4 force-commanded nails**. Each nail sits on a compliant leaf (0.3 N/mm, finger-like, §3.12) whose root is driven by a small actuator with ≥ 10 mm travel (2 mm for 0–0.6 N, plus lift) and ≥ 15 Hz bandwidth.

```
   carrier (x, y, yaw) ═══════════════════════════════
     │ act1      │ act2      │ act3      │ act4       ← voice coil / QDD gimbal / micro-servo (prototype)
     ╪ leaf      ╪ leaf      ╪ leaf      ╪ leaf       k ≈ 0.3 N/mm, hard stop ≤ 0.6 N
     ◣ nail      ◣ nail      ◣ nail      ◣ nail       20 mm pitch, 45°
   ~~~~~~~~~~~~~ hair / scalp ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
   each f_i(t) replays its recorded envelope: landing spread, ±30–50 % modulation, unload at reversal
```

**(c) Sensation link.** §7 rank 6 (independent asynchrony, which red team 1 calls "the one thing the carrier fixes"), rank 3 (force, now with its recorded per-stroke modulation) and DR4 unloading at reversal done per finger with recorded timing. It also lets P4 "spider" be emulated by out-of-phase force oscillation at 3–6 Hz.

**(d) Plausibility.** The mechanical power per finger is F·v ≈ 0.6 N × (2π · 15 Hz · 1 mm) ≈ 0.06 W, which is trivial. A moving-coil actuator at ~1–2 N/A needs 0.3–0.6 A `[EST]`. SG90-class servos on the leaf root (red team 1's $10 option) reach only about 5–8 Hz `[EST]`. That is fine as a prototype, but it will smear the 20–80 ms landing structure. A force cap of a leaf plus a hard stop keeps safety mechanical.

**(e) Cheapest experiment (~$15, one weekend, after D2).** Fit three SG90s at the leaf roots of the SP1 hand. Replay one recorded segment with (i) the recorded per-finger force envelopes and (ii) a constant force with the same mean. Rate them blind.

**(f) Replaces / combines:** it competes with the porcupine's pin and selector. Per-pin force actuators on dozens of pins are not credible, so if D4 wins, the architecture swings back toward a moving four-finger hand (Teams B and E). The porcupine's coverage could survive as a "hand that walks" on its shell.

**(g) Why it might not work.** Hair-plowing noise (tangential spikes to 0.3 N, §3.5) may mask per-finger force modulation. Then constant-force pins are perceptually equivalent, and the porcupine's passive force reference is the correct choice. That is exactly what (e) tests.

### D5 — The live puppeteer: replay with the human still in the loop

**(a) Assumption broken:** the device is autonomous, and component E (social attribution, §1.1) is not reproducible.

**(b) Principle.** The D2 capture rig runs in real time as a master, and the device is the slave. A partner scratches the instrumented mannequin head, or wears the IMU/FSR rings on bare air ("air-scratching"). The device reproduces the motion on Michael within 80 ms while the partner watches Michael's reactions. The same hardware plays recordings when no one is there.

**(c) Sensation link.** It restores the responsiveness that replay loses (D1 risk 2) and the attribution that changes how touch is felt. In Gazzola et al. 2012, S1 responses to *identical* caresses changed with the toucher the subject believed was responsible ([PNAS](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22665808/)). It moves component E from "not reproducible" (§1.3) to "sometimes present".

**(d) Plausibility.** Latency budget: IMU/FSR ~10 ms, radio ~10 ms, device tracking 30–50 ms; total about 50–80 ms `[EST]`. The partner cannot feel the hair directly, so a mannequin with real hair is better than air-scratching.

**(e) Cheapest experiment ($0, once SP1 runs).** Run the same machine pattern under three framings: "a program", "a recording of [helper]" and "[helper] live from the next room". Compare the ratings. A large framing effect is the cheapest possible sensation gain.

**(f) Replaces / combines:** it is a second mode of D1 and D2. It needs no extra hardware.

**(g) Why it might not work.** Solo use is the point of the device, and a partner who is present could just scratch. The value is remote use, which is niche, plus D2 data collection. Framing effects may fade once Michael knows the setup.

### 3.3 Pipeline (offline Python, then a firmware player)

1. **Sync.** The helper raps a knuckle on the plate at the start and end of each block. The spike appears in the load cells, the IMUs, the audio and the video, and drift is corrected linearly between the two spikes.
2. **Reconstruct.**
   - Camera: nail dots → MediaPipe / colour blobs → stereo 3D → head frame (headband markers + photogrammetry ellipsoid) → (θ, φ, region, lie-relative direction).
   - IMU: high-band energy → per-finger contact flags; this also gives the §3.14 contact fraction as a proxy.
   - Load cells: high-pass 0.5 Hz → total scratch force; low-pass → lean-in.
   - FSR ratios × total → f_i(t).
3. **Segment** at lift-offs (all f_i < 0.03 N for > 150 ms). Label primitives P1–P7 by rules (stroke length, frequency, finger phase), then fix the labels by hand.
4. **Analyse** what decides whether D1 is a sensation leap or only a speed leap:
   - stroke-to-stroke autocorrelation and DFA exponent α of force, duration and length (α ≈ 0.5 means white, so refit §4.4 and stop; α → 1 means 1/f, so keep replay);
   - cross-correlations between variables;
   - IAAFT surrogates: same spectrum and marginals, scrambled phase, for blind machine tests.
5. **Store** a `scratch_roll` per segment: `t, x, y, yaw, f1..f4, contact1..4, region, primitive` at 200 Hz, with Michael's knob/button stream aligned.
6. **Retarget** to the device: IK on the device's surface model, servo lag pre-compensation, per-finger force → actuator command, or the mean force if the hardware is passive.
7. **Clamp** in firmware, always: F ≤ 0.6 N per contact, v ≤ 20 cm/s, unload ≥ 50 % at every reversal (DR4), lift every ≤ 20 s (DR7). The mechanical caps stay.
8. **Play** through the D3 bandit, logging device readback (position and current) at 200 Hz. The **replay error per channel** against the recording becomes the device's regression test.

## 4. Honest verdict: a leap in sensation, or only in development speed?

**Development speed: certainly, and by a lot.** One afternoon and about $130 replace roughly fifteen ESTIMATED rows with measurements on Michael's own head. It also resolves the program's largest internal disagreement (per-nail force, a factor of 2–3) and gives every future build an objective target: the replay error between the machine and the recording, per channel. That alone is worth more than any single mechanism change, because every architecture so far was designed to numbers that may be wrong on every axis at once (hybrid §2.1).

**Sensation: conditional.** Replay changes only component C (§1.1). It does nothing for A (near-root hair sweep) or B (edge on skin), which are per-stroke physics set by the tip. Replay is a sensation leap only if human pattern structure beats parametric jitter: HN ≫ SN in the 2×2, and α well above 0.5 in the analysis. I put that at about 40 %. The prior is real, because §7 ranks irregularity as the most important and least known variable, but the scripted hand may well be indistinguishable `[EST]`.

The **closed loop (D3)** is the more probable sensation gain, at about 65 % `[EST]`. Even with no replay at all, setting force, speed and region dwell to Michael's measured preferences, and keeping them moving while they work, attacks the variables that most likely sit furthest from optimum today.

**Hardware changes replay forces:**

1. **A per-contact force channel at ≥ 10–15 Hz**, if the D4 test shows envelopes matter. This is incompatible with passive constant-force pins and swings the architecture toward a moving four-finger hand.
2. **A yaw/direction DOF**, because recordings will change direction relative to the lie (§5).
3. **Head registration and a head-shape model of Michael**, so recordings retarget to the right place.
4. **Readback sensing and logging on the device** (position, current or force, at 200 Hz), so fidelity is measurable.
5. **A grounded frame of reference** if lean-in is to be a reward. This is one more argument for frame-mounted over head-worn.
6. microSD storage and one timebase.

## 5. Best bet

**D2 run as the gate, feeding D3 on top of a D1 library.** In practice: one afternoon with the best scratcher Michael knows, on a load-cell face cradle, with IMU rings, a 240 fps phone and a rating knob, and with the human-hand 2×2 inside the session.

The case: it is the only leap on the list that costs under $150, needs no motor and cannot fail to produce value. The worst outcome is that it converts the program's estimates into Michael-specific measurements and settles the force-window dispute. The 2×2 then tells the program, for $0, where the remaining gap between machine and human actually lives:

- **in the tip** (HN ≫ HW): go back to edge physics, not mechanisms;
- **in the pattern** (HN ≫ SN): replay and the closed loop become the product's core, and per-finger force hardware (D4) earns its place;
- **neither**: build the simplest rig and personalise it with a button.

Each future build would then be judged by one number, its replay error against a real human on Michael's real head, rather than by argument from estimates. That is the step change: not a new mechanism, but a ground truth.
