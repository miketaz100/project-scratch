Project Scratch · Packaging decision

Where the Drive Lives

The scratching hardware has two parts. The light part, the halo and the pad with its air pins, has to be on your head. The heavy, noisy part drives it. This page shows three places that heavy part could go, drawn on the same seated person.

What the heavy part is
  • The circle-and-line drive: two small motors turning two cranks, which push three syringes. Their air lines make the pad copy the cranks' motion.
  • An air pump and a small bottle as a reservoir, plus a relief valve.
  • One fast valve per pin, 8 to 16 of them, each about 10 g.
  • The controller and its power supply.

Together this is roughly the size of a shoebox and weighs an estimated 0.7 to 1 kg. The pump is the noisiest piece, about as loud as a quiet fridge at a metre.

Twice the 500 g safety limit for head-borne weight, and the pump and motors buzz straight into the skull, the noise every round-3 agent warned about. Not viable with today's parts.

Recommendation

Make the chair the all-in-one. The drive box mounts on the back of a high-back chair, behind the headrest, and the helmet docks on the chair's wing with its single bundle already plugged in. You never handle a separate box or a loose tangle of tubes: you sit down, lift the helmet off its hook, and start. The head carries only the light halo and pad, the noise stays behind you, and every heavy part is easy to reach while we iterate.

Design the box so it can shrink into option B later. Once the prototype tells us how many pins and how much air are really needed, the same parts can be repackaged into a neck unit. If you would rather start wearable, option B works, at the cost of pump noise near the ears and a kilogram on the shoulders.
