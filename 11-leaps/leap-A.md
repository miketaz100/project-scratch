## 0. The frame every design shares, and why I doubt it

Every architecture so far (arm rake, crown hand, porcupine) is built around one stimulus: **a stiff nail edge at 0.3–0.5 N sliding ~30 mm on scalp skin at 5–15 cm/s.** Five assumptions sit underneath it, and none has been tested:

1. **Hair is the obstacle.** Yet scratch-model §1.3 ranks near-root hair sweep as pleasure contributor #1 and the nail edge as #2.
2. **A stroke has one speed.** But the pleasant optimum (~3 cm/s) and the satisfying-scratch band (11–18 cm/s) don't overlap.
3. **A stroke is a tip that physically travels.**
4. **Variety means jitter inside one channel.** Nothing alternates *what kind* of stimulus the scalp gets.
5. **The scalp is a neutral target.** The only hard data on scratching pleasure says it tracks **itch** (bin Saif 2012, prior-art §3.1).

### 0.1 What the recent literature adds (and two corrections to the provocation)

**Two corrections to the provocation.** (1) The seconds-long after-discharge belongs to **CT** fibres and is most robust after plucking. Aβ hair units fire only while hair moves. So "letting it ring" is a bet on the CT channel, and that channel also fatigues. (2) The evidence does **not** show hair deflection is the *dominant* channel. Hair deflection is *sufficient* to drive affective afferents (Moore 2025), but Aβ is *necessary* for pleasantness (PMC10226526). The defensible thesis is narrower: **the scalp is a multi-channel organ, and every design so far plays one channel.**

## LEAP 1 — ROOT-WAVE: make the hair the instrument, and make the nail the seasoning

**(a) Assumption broken.** That the pleasure comes from the edge on skin and that hair is an obstacle on the way there.

**(b) Principle.** A second kind of end-effector whose job is to bend hairs **at the root**, not to load the skin: a staggered row of fine, smooth, round-ended flexible tines (0.5–0.65 mm nylon monofilament, flame-rounded, or 0.4 mm polished spring wire with a 0.5 mm fused tip). Each tine rides on the skin at only 10–30 mN, so it sits in the hair lanes at the follicle exit. The tines oscillate laterally 6–10 mm with a phase lag between neighbours, so a **wave front of follicle bending travels across the patch** at 3–6 cm/s (the pleasantness peak) while each tine moves locally at 5–12 cm/s. Nail contacts become a minority instrument (20–40 % of contact time) for the "reach the itch" moments.

```
  side view                                  top view: phase-lagged tines -> travelling wave
   carrier bar ──────────────────            t0:  |  /  /  |  \  \  |  /      wave front
     │  │  │  │  │  │   flexible tines           ●  ●  ●  ●  ●  ●  ●  ●   (8 mm pitch, 2 rows
     │  │  │  │  │  │   0.5 mm nylon          t1:  /  |  \  \  |  /  /  |     staggered 4 mm)
  ~~~╲~~╲~~╲~~╲~~╲~~╲~~~ hair canopy           ──────────────►  apparent motion 3–6 cm/s
  ════●══●══●══●══●══●═══ scalp: tips at the follicle exit, 10–30 mN each
       ↔ 6–10 mm local sweep, 3–5 Hz
```

**(c) Why it could be a step change.** Per-hair follicle drive scales as 1/L² with engagement height (scratch-model §2.3): a tip at the skin bends each hair at L ≈ 0–2 mm, 4–25× more than a brush working 10 mm up. A nail does the same, but only along 3–5 lines of 4 mm each. Rough numbers (EST): 16 tines × 1 mm effective width × (2 × 8 mm × 4 Hz = 64 mm/s) ≈ 10 cm²/s swept × 200 hairs/cm² ≈ **2,000 root deflections/s, ×2 counting spring-back ≈ 4,000/s.** That matches the human four-nail figure (~4,000/s) at **one-third of the total force** (0.32 N vs 0.8–1.2 N), and it avoids the nail's "reach the skin" problem (redteam-1 "riding the pile"). It moves §7 #2 (root engagement: from 40–70 % of stroke time to roughly 100 %) and #6 (contacts: from 3–5 to 16+, each compliant and on its own phase). It is the wire spider, the one device users call "tingly", with its two flaws removed: 2–4 mm beads that ride on the hair (above the ~0.8 mm crossover, hair-interaction §2.1), and a single slow splay instead of a continuous wave.

**(d) Plausibility.** Nylon 0.5 mm, E ≈ 2.5 GPa, free length 25 mm: k = 3EI/L³ ≈ 3·2.5e9·3.1e-15/1.56e-5 ≈ 1.5 N/m, so a 15 mm preload deflection gives ~22 mN (EST). That sits right in the target, and force varies by only ±7 mN per ±5 mm of seating error. This tine is also its own force reference, which answers the head-worn force problem (judge-3) for this channel at once. Drive: one bar per row on a small crank or servo, ~5 g moving mass. Hair safety: pitch ≥ 8 mm per row (scratch-model failure mode (c): tines < 5 mm pitch act as a comb), no apertures, open-ended, lift every 10–20 s (DR7). Long hair at 3–5 Hz reciprocation is the open risk.

**(e) Cheapest experiment (≤ $10, two evenings, helper).** *Night 1, motorless:* random order, 30 s each, 3 blocks. Michael says pleasure and scratchiness (0–10 each) and any shivers aloud into a phone:
- N: nails at normal scratching force;
- R: the same hand motion with the nails *barely grazing*, "moving the hairs at the roots, not pressing" (this is scratch-model §9 Q1 by hand);
- C: fingertip pulps over the canopy only (tip deflection).
*Night 2:* a $6 "root rake" (12 stubs of 0.65 mm trimmer line, 25 mm free, two staggered rows at 8 mm pitch in a printed bar, tips flame-rounded). The helper wiggles it 6–10 mm at ~4 Hz while drifting, vs N.
**Decision rule:** if R or the root rake scores ≥ N on pleasure and at least 60 % of N on scratchiness, the hair channel deserves its own hardware.

**(f) Effect on the architecture.** The porcupine's "dozens of nail pins" splits in two: **6–10 nail pins** (channel B) and **whisker clusters** (channel A). Whiskers need no latch: a cluster that is not driven is just lying in the hair at 20 mN, and that is close to hair-safe. The selector then chooses *channel* as well as location. In any non-helmet design, the root rake is a second tool on the same carrier.

**(g) Why it might not work.** Forearm data cut against it: depilation and CT suppression barely move pleasantness (Jönsson 2017; Nikesjö 2026). Light hair movement can read as **tickle or creepy-crawly itch** rather than pleasure (Fukuoka 2013). It may read as "a brush," which is pleasant but not a scratch. Fine tines reciprocating in 20 cm hair are the most snag-prone part of the idea.

## LEAP 2 — CARRIER + GRAIN: two timescales at once, so both velocity optima are served

**(a) Assumption broken.** That a stroke has one velocity. Every design picks one (50–150 mm/s), and the porcupine's selector is effectively still (5–20 s dwell per group).

**(b) Principle.** Superimpose a fast, short **grain** (each contact scrubs 6–10 mm peak-to-peak at 4–6 Hz, local slip 5–12 cm/s mean and ~8–19 cm/s peak, nail edge on skin) on a slow, long **carrier** (the whole contact group migrates 2–5 cm/s along a 6–15 cm path: nape → crown, temple → crown). This is what a person's two hands do in a salon shampoo: fingers wiggle while the hands travel. It is primitive P4 (spider, "strongest tingle driver", §4.1) riding on P2 (sweep, "luxurious").

```
  position on scalp
   ^                                   . . grain: 6–10 mm, 4–6 Hz, local slip 5–12 cm/s
   |                        /\/\/\/\/\/          (Aβ field/hair units, nail edge: "satisfying")
   |               /\/\/\/\/
   |      /\/\/\/\/          ── carrier: 2–5 cm/s over 6–15 cm
   | /\/\/                       (centrally inferred velocity: "pleasant")
   +---------------------------------------------> time (2–7 s per traverse)
```

**(c) Why it could be a step change.** Pleasantness follows **inferred global velocity**: Pehkonen 2025 and Le 2025 find the same inverted-U for apparent motion and Aβ-only strokes with local drive held fixed. The "satisfying scratch" is a **local** event: a 100–300 kPa edge sliding at 10–18 cm/s (prior-art §3.1). A single-speed device must compromise (the porcupine's 50–150 mm/s). Carrier + grain gives local slip of ~10 cm/s and global velocity of ~3–5 cm/s **at the same time**. It moves §7 #4 (velocity becomes dual-band) and #1 (two oscillators at an irrational frequency ratio never repeat a tuple, which satisfies §4.4's rule for free). Physical bonuses:
- The against-grain hair wave needs ~25 mm to build (hair-interaction §2.3). With 6–10 mm excursions it never forms, so **against-grain grain is allowed**, and that lifts hairs at the follicle.
- Each spot sees grain for only ≈ (5 + 8 mm)/30 mm/s ≈ 0.4 s (2–3 passes). That automatically meets the abrasion rule of ≤ 50 passes/min per spot (3.24) and the CT fatigue rule (no revisit for ≥ 5–10 s).

**(d) Plausibility.** Shaking a 120 g porcupine shell at ±4 mm and 5 Hz takes only ~0.47 N peak (a = 3.9 m/s²), but the helmet would shake at 5 Hz unless two half-shells run in antiphase. It is better to drive the grain with a $5 N20 eccentric (3–5 mm offset, WR-1 style) on a 10–20 g contact bar, and let the servo or selector supply the carrier. Tangential load is ≈ 0.1 N, while moving the scalp 5–15 mm takes newtons (§1.2). So skin translation stays under 1 mm and more than 85 % of the excursion is true slip (EST): a scratch, not a knead. The unload at each reversal (DR4) has to come from geometry, e.g. a ~1 mm-tall elliptical grain path from two eccentrics.

**(e) Cheapest experiment ($0, one evening, helper).** Randomized blocks, 20 s each, 3 blocks:
- (1) P1 rake in place, 30 mm, ~2.5 Hz;
- (2) P2 slow sweep at ~3 cm/s;
- (3) **carrier + grain**: fingers wiggle ~8 mm at ~5 Hz while the hand travels ~3 cm/s from hairline over the crown to the occiput;
- (4) grain in place (no carrier).
Rate pleasure and scratchiness separately, and record "how long until it got boring" in (1) and (4). **Prediction:** (3) wins pleasure, ties (1) on scratchiness, and (4) fades fastest. *Follow-up ($8):* N20 gearmotor + printed eccentric + 3-nail bar on a hand-held handle. The helper supplies only the slow carrier, which tests *machine* grain.

**(f) Effect on the architecture.** This inverts the porcupine's two motions. The **shell stroke shrinks** from 25–35 mm at 1–2.5 Hz to 6–10 mm at 4–6 Hz (smaller, lighter, and the shell's guidance problem on a sphere nearly disappears). The **selector speeds up** from a 5–20 s dwell to a continuous walk with group hand-off every 0.2–0.3 s. For the earlier rake designs it means: yaw/arm servo = carrier, a $5 eccentric = grain.

**(g) Why it might not work.** At small amplitude and higher frequency it can drift toward "buzz" or a rub (scratch-model §8 item 5 gate: > 20 Hz is vibration, so stay ≤ 6–7 Hz and ≥ 5 mm). Unlifted micro-reversals in long hair can start loops. A fast selector hand-off is a hard mechanism in the porcupine. And people may want the long, slow, single P2 sweep *as well*, so grain must be switchable off.

## LEAP 3 — RELAY: build strokes out of sequenced stationary cells, and schedule them around fatigue and after-discharge

**(a) Assumption broken.** That a stroke needs a tip that physically travels across the head, and that pattern means trajectory.

**(b) Principle.** Cover the scalp with **40–60 independent "scratch cells"** at 30–40 mm pitch. Each cell can deliver one short local nail flick: 6–10 mm, 120–250 ms, 0.2–0.4 N. Firmware creates strokes by **firing cells in sequence** (apparent motion). A nape-to-forehead stroke 25 cm long at 5 cm/s is eight cells fired 0.6–0.8 s apart with overlapping flicks. Then a **refractory schedule** is enforced: no cell's patch is re-fired within 10–20 s (CT fatigue), each bout is followed by 0.5–2 s of **silence on that patch** (let any CT after-discharge be felt, unmasked), and the next bout starts somewhere chosen by a **surprise budget**: about 70 % continue the line, 20 % jump to a far region, 10 % pause.

```
  scalp map (top view), cells ● at 35 mm; one relay stroke fired 1→8, 0.7 s apart
              forehead
         ●     ●  8  ●     ●
      ●     ●     7     ●     ●        each cell:  [flexure]──nail
      ●     ●     6     ●     ●                     ↑ 6–10 mm flick, 150 ms
      ●     ●     5     ●     ●                     SMA wire (silent), cools 1–2 s
      ●     ●     4     ●     ●                     -> refractory 10–20 s = physiology's own
         ●     ●  3  ●     ●
            ●     2     ●
                  1  nape
```

**(c) Why it could be a step change.** It makes strokes a hand cannot: one perceived 20–30 cm sweep across the vault. It makes **location** unpredictable on every bout, the cheapest dishabituator (§4.3, §9 Q5). And it respects receptor timescales that P1 rakes break by re-stroking the same CT field every 0.25–0.5 s. Apparent motion at up to 40 mm spacing reads as continuous and pleasant (SHIFTS; best at 5.1 cm/s), with the same pleasantness tuning as real motion (Pehkonen 2025). Scalp two-point acuity is roughly 15–40 mm (prior-art §3.3), so 35 mm cells sit at the edge of resolution, which is what lets them blend into a line. §7 moved: #1 (location becomes free), #13 (whole head), #14 (dwell drops from 5–20 s per patch to < 1 s per patch, followed by ≥ 10 s rest).

**(d) Plausibility.** **Shape-memory-alloy** wire fits neatly. SMA is slow to cool (~1 s for 0.1 mm wire, EST; verify against Flexinol data), but the physiology *wants* each cell idle for ≥ 10 s, so that weakness costs nothing. A 0.10 mm wire pulls ~1.5 N at 3–4 % strain. 60 mm of wire through a 3:1 flexure lever gives a ~7 mm flick at ~0.45 N tangential, against ~0.1 N friction. Normal force comes from a passive 0.02 N/mm spring (pin-unit's). Each cell is about 2–3 g (~150 g for 50 cells). It is **silent**, with no gears and no rotation. Each flick draws ~200 mA for 150 ms, with 3–5 cells live at once (< 1 A). The hazard is that the wire runs at 70–90 °C. It must sit on the outer face of the carrier, ≥ 5 mm from the scalp, insulated, with a thermal fuse. Micro solenoids or 1.5 g servos would also work but are noisier.

**(e) Cheapest experiment ($0 + $15).** *Motorless, helper:* the helper uses four fingers of both hands to place **one-centimetre nail flicks** in sequence along a line from nape to crown, ~3–4 cm apart, one flick every ~0.25–0.7 s. Ask: "one continuous stroke or separate taps? how pleasant?" Compare with a real continuous stroke along the same line. Second probe, the "ring" test: after a 3 s bout, the helper either moves straight on or holds off for 1.5 s before restarting elsewhere. Which feels better? *Bench ($15):* one SMA cell (Flexinol sample, printed flexure, MOSFET on an Arduino) to measure flick length, time, cooling and surface temperature.

**(f) Effect on the architecture.** This replaces the porcupine's **moving inner shell and slow selector** with a **static shell of independent cells**. Selection *is* actuation. It could combine with Leap 1 (some cells carry whisker tines) and Leap 2 (adjacent cells fired with overlapping timing give a carrier, and the flicks are the grain).

**(g) Why it might not work.** The apparent-motion evidence is from forearm skin-slip tactors, not nail flicks through hair, and on the scalp the cells may read as **pecks**. The fix is for the nail to rest in contact at low force so a flick only adds tangential travel (redteam-1's "tapping" attack). SMA fatigue life (~10⁵–10⁶ cycles), heat and a 50-wire harness are real costs. A stroke with no hand behind it may feel uncanny.

## LEAP 4 — POLYPHONIC HEAD: rotate *channels*, not just jitter one, and treat sound as one of them

**(a) Assumption broken.** That one modality (a hard edge at constant-ish force) with stochastic jitter can avoid habituation and read as a person. A person's hand is a multi-channel instrument: nails (edge, Aβ field/SA1/Aδ), pulps ruffling hair (hair units, CT), the **warm weight of a resting palm** (SA1/SA2 pressure, warmth), pauses, and a characteristic dry hiss.

**(b) Principle.** A **channel scheduler** above the pattern engine. Channels:
- **N** nail edge (0.2–0.5 N);
- **H** root-wave tines (Leap 1, 0.02 N each);
- **P** palm: a broad 40 × 60 mm silicone pad held at 33–35 °C on a soft spring, resting 1–3 N (≈ 0.5–1 kPa), still or drifting 1 cm/s;
- **S** silence (contacts lifted or resting with no motion);
- **A** audio: the natural hiss left unmasked by a silent drive, optionally near-field scratch sound that comes 100–200 ms before touch on the matching side.
Rules: switch channel every 3–15 s (jittered); no channel more than ~40 % of any 60 s window; a channel returns to the same patch only after its refractory time (CT-rich channels H and P ≥ 15 s).

```
  time ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────►
  N  ████████      ███             ██████████          ████
  H          ██████    ███████                ███████       ███
  P                               ████              ████
  S               ▒                      ▒▒                ▒
  A  (hiss tied to N/H; optional lead-in tone 150 ms before each new region)
```

**(c) Why it could be a step change.** Adaptation is **per afferent class**. Pacinian fade happens in under 1 s, SA1 within seconds, CT fatigue lasts seconds to minutes (Vallbo 1999), and central satiety sets in within minutes, most at the most pleasant setting (Triscoli 2014; PMC12189115: decline within ~3–5 min). Jitter inside one channel slows *central* habituation but cannot rest a fatigued *peripheral* class. Switching channels rests one class while another works, and the novelty itself dishabituates. The Aβ-block result says pressure-type Aβ input is part of pleasantness, and a palm is its cheapest source. CT firing and pleasantness peak at **skin temperature (~32 °C)** and fall at 42 °C (Ackerley 2014, [J Neurosci](https://www.jneurosci.org/content/34/8/2879)). So the palm should match the skin, not feel "heated": its job is to remove the cold-machine cue. On sound, cutting motor noise may be worth as much as a tip change (Zampini 2003). A near-field cue just before touch builds the expectation ASMR depends on (PPH 2025). §7 moved: #1 gains a *categorical* dimension; #15–16 (sound, thermal) go from confounds to controlled channels; resting-palm pressure is new, and no design has it.

**(d) Plausibility.** Palm pad: 15–25 g with a 3–5 W polyimide heater and a thermistor (scalp ~33–34 °C, so it needs only a few watts). It hangs from a soft 0.1 N/mm spring and can live on the porcupine's outer-shell comfort pad position or on a crown band. H tines: Leap 1. Audio: a $10 bone-conduction pad, or nothing, provided the drive is quiet (gimbal motors, SMA, or slow servos). Software: a 4-state Markov scheduler, ~150 lines.

**(e) Cheapest experiment ($0, two evenings, helper).** Two 6-minute sessions in counterbalanced order: A = nails only, irregular (§4.4); B = shuffled rotation of nails ~8 s / pulps ruffling hair ~6 s / warm palm resting ~4 s / nothing ~2 s. Michael says a 0–10 rating every 30 s. Compare minutes 4–6 and the slope. **Dishabituation probe** in A: after 3 min of nails, 10 s of palm, then nails again. Does the rating rebound ≥ 1.5? Run one minute of each session with foam ear plugs to measure the share due to sound.

**(f) Effect on the architecture.** The porcupine selector becomes a **channel × location** selector. The outer shell gains an active palm. Drive choice gets a new criterion: audible noise at the ear below the hair hiss (scratch-model §8 item 12, today "UNSURE acceptable"), which favours SMA, gimbal or slow drives over geared servos running fast.

**(g) Why it might not work.** It drifts toward "massage robot", and the program's identity is scratching. Hardware grows by one or two effector types. A fixed rotation becomes its own predictable rhythm unless the scheduler is stochastic. The thermal and audio channels may be too small to notice. If Michael's ratings are flat over 6 minutes of nails only (he may be one of the third who don't habituate), the leap buys nothing for him.

## LEAP 5 — TEASE-AND-RELIEVE: manufacture a small itch, then scratch it

**(a) Assumption broken.** That the scalp is a neutral target and the device's only job is to deliver good touch.

**(b) Principle.** The measured driver of scratching pleasure is **itch intensity**. Pleasure tracked itch and faded as it subsided (bin Saif 2012). Light lateral hair movement creates **mechanical itch or tickle** through a dedicated Aβ hair-afferent pathway (Fukuoka 2013; Fatima 2026). So: a whisper-light **tease** on a 1–2 cm patch, 0.5–2 s of hair flutter with nothing pressing the skin (fine fibre at 2–5 mm above the skin, swung 2–4 mm at 5–30 Hz). After a 0.3–1.5 s delay it is followed by a **nail scratch on exactly that patch**. The delay is the anticipation, and its length is randomized.

```
   tease (fibre flutter, no skin load)      gap 0.3–1.5 s       relief (nail, 0.3 N, 1–2 s)
   ~~~~╲╱╲╱~~~~  hairs swing               ...anticipation...   ════▶ edge on skin, same patch
   ════════════  skin untouched                                  ════════════
```

**(c) Why it could be a step change.** It turns a pleasant touch into **itch relief**, the only kind of scratching whose pleasure has been measured as strong. It adds a cue → wait → relief loop, which is what makes a person's scratch feel *responsive* ("they found the spot"). The new variable is itch at scratch onset (0 → a mild 2–3/10). The expected gain is in **satisfaction** (component B) more than tingle.

**(d) Plausibility.** The tease element is tiny: a 0.1–0.2 mm nylon fibre or a natural hair in a holder on a 6 mm coin vibration motor, or the Leap-1 tines run at 0 normal force and higher frequency. Hairline, temple, behind-ear and nape (vellus-rich, scratch-model §5 "treats") are the likeliest itch sites. Over terminal occipital hair it may produce tickle instead.

**(e) Cheapest experiment ($3, one evening, helper).** The helper uses a size-0 round paintbrush tip or a single long hair to flutter the hairs on a small patch for ~2 s without touching the skin. Then, at random:
- (i) a 2 s nail scratch on the same patch after ~0.5–1 s;
- (ii) the same scratch on a patch 5 cm away;
- (iii) the scratch with no tease.
12 trials, sites rotated across nape, behind-ear, hairline and occiput. Michael rates itch before the scratch (0–10) and satisfaction after it (0–10). **Decision rule:** (i) beats (iii) by ≥ 1.5 points at two or more sites.

**(f) Effect on the architecture.** It adds a tease element ahead of some nail contacts, or reuses the H channel. The firmware gains a coupled two-event primitive.

**(g) Why it might not work.** Itch can linger or spread (alloknesis) and become annoying. It is idiosyncratic. On terminal scalp hair it may only tickle, and tickle followed by a scratch may feel like being *messed with*. Ethically trivial, but it must be opt-in and never the default.

## Best bet: LEAP 2, CARRIER + GRAIN

It is the only leap that resolves the program's central sensory contradiction instead of choosing a side. The literature puts the pleasant optimum at ~3 cm/s and the satisfying scratch at 11–18 cm/s, and the porcupine, like every earlier design, splits the difference with one stroke speed. Two 2025 studies (Pehkonen et al.; Le et al.) show that pleasantness follows the **inferred global** velocity, computed centrally, even when the local afferent drive is held fixed. So a device can give the nail edge its satisfying local slip and give the brain a slow, pleasant global motion at the same moment. That is what the best human head-scratch, the salon shampoo, does. Several physical gains come with it: short excursions never build the against-grain hair wave, so against-grain becomes usable; no spot gets more than 2–3 passes, so abrasion and CT fatigue are handled automatically; two incommensurate oscillators give non-repeating patterns without a random-number generator. It also *simplifies* the porcupine: a shell stroke of 6–10 mm instead of 25–35 mm. The cost is a faster selector (the hard part). It can be tested tonight with a helper's hands at $0, and with an $8 eccentric grain head this weekend. If the helper's "shampoo scratch" does not beat both the rake and the sweep on Michael's head, it should be dropped before anyone draws a part. If it does win, Leap 1 (root tines as the grain's contact for part of the time) and Leap 4 (rotating channels on top) are the natural next layers, and Leap 3 (SMA relay cells) is the bold long-term version of the same idea, in which the carrier is made entirely in firmware.
