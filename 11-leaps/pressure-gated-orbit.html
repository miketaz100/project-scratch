Pressure-Gated Orbit

Two ideas from the actuation agent that work together on the porcupine helmet. Air pins decide which nails touch and how hard. A shell that circles without ever stopping supplies the motion. Each pin picks the slice of every circle during which it presses down, and that choice is what makes the stroke.

The idea in four sentences
  1. Every pin is a small air piston. Air pushes it down onto the scalp; venting the air lets it spring back up out of the hair.
  2. Because the push is air pressure times piston area, the force is the same no matter how the helmet sits on your head.
  3. The shell holding all the pins slides around a small circle, about 4 cm across, continuously and always in the same direction.
  4. A pin that is down for one slice of the circle draws a short scratch in one direction. Picking a different slice changes the direction, length and timing of that pin's scratch, so every pin can do something different while one motor does all the moving.

Part 1: the air pin

The porcupine pin as designed so far uses a 45 mm soft spring to set its force, a latch to hold it up, and a selector motor to release it. The air pin replaces all three. A thin rubber bladder sits in a 7 mm bore in the shell, above a free-sliding plastic pin carrying the nail. A thin tube runs from the top of the bore to a box on your desk.

Why the force stays the same however the helmet sits

A spring pushes harder the more it is squashed, so if the helmet sits a few millimetres lower, every spring pin presses harder. That is the problem that forced the 45 mm springs. An air pin connected to a pressure rail pushes with pressure times area wherever the pin happens to be: 10 kPa on a 7 mm bore is 0.39 N at any extension. With three rails, firmware can give each pin a different force on every stroke, which is how a real hand varies finger pressure. If power is cut, every valve falls back to vent and a dump valve empties the reservoir, so all pins lift on their own.

Part 2: the circling shell

In the porcupine as designed, the shell rakes back and forth, so it has to stop and reverse twice per stroke, and every pin must be lifted at each reversal. The orbit removes that. The shell slides around a circle about 20 mm in radius without rotating, like your hand polishing a table. It never stops, never reverses, and runs at a steady speed: 1 circle per second is about 126 mm/s.

A pin pressed down for a slice of the circle scratches along that slice. The slice decides everything about the stroke:

Because the shell never reverses, no pin ever reverses while touching the scalp. Each pin lands while moving, drags, and lifts while still moving, which is the hair-safe rule every earlier design had to engineer around.

One circle, step by step

  1. The shell is at the bottom of its circle, moving right. All pins are lifted.
  2. The shell reaches the right side and starts moving forward. Firmware opens pin A's valve to the 10 kPa rail; 40 ms later it opens pin B's on the 14 kPa rail. Both nails land while already moving.
  3. For the next 60° of circle, about 170 ms, both nails drag forward through the hair, about 20 mm.
  4. Pin A's valve vents and it lifts; pin B follows 30 ms later. Pin C, whose slice this circle was placed elsewhere, does a short diagonal flick on its own.
  5. On the left side of the circle the shell is moving backward, and the same pins can land again for the return scratch, with new timings and forces.
  6. Next circle, firmware draws fresh slices for every pin. Nothing ever repeats exactly.

What it adds: a bundle of tubes about 1 to 2 cm thick from the helmet to the desk, and a desk box with a pump, a bottle, regulators and one small valve per pin. At 40 pins the agent estimates $250 to $400.

What could go wrong

  • Too slow. If valves and tubes take more than about 80 ms, pins land late and lift late. Fix: move the valves closer, to a pack on the shoulders.
  • Lifted pins stirring hair. If a lifted pin stays inside the hair while the shell circles, it stirs hair in loops. Pins must lift fully clear, 15 to 25 mm, which air does easily.
  • Friction. Bladder and bore friction could eat 0.05 to 0.1 N of a 0.4 N target.
  • The tube bundle. Fine at a desk or on a sofa, not portable.
  • Buzz. Force must come from switching between fixed rails, never from flickering a valve, or the nail vibrates.
  • Circling feel. If strokes read as little circles rather than scratches, slices are kept to 60° or less.

The cheap test

Under $40 and a weekend: three air pins in a small printed block, a $3 pump, three $3 valves and the controller we already specified. First, on a kitchen scale, check that force stays flat as the pin extends, and film the landing at 240 fps to measure the delay. Then hold the block in your hand and scratch your own head while firmware randomly varies when each pin lands and how hard, against all pins locked down. That one comparison answers the deciding question: does varying each finger's landing and pressure, on top of one shared motion, make it feel like a hand? A second small test puts the block on a circling plate over paper with inked tips, to see the strokes drawn.
