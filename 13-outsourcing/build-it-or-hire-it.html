Build It or Hire It

Seven ways to get the lean design onto your head, compared on cash, your own hours, time to the first session, and the chance of a good session. Costs are for the lean design you approved on 2 October: dish-gate pad, cable drive, hand-moved stations, one pad.

The answer first
  • Every route has roughly the same chance of a good session within six months, about 20 to 32 %. Two things cap it. First, whether the design feels good on your scalp is about 40 % whoever builds it. Second, hiring does not shorten the calendar.
  • What money buys is your time back (about $190 to $250 per hour saved), a better chance of finishing within a year, an independent safety check, and professional CAD you own for a later version or product. It does not buy a better feel or a faster first session.
  • The estimator's default: pay an engineer for a design review only, build it yourself, and pay for a short safety check before the first session on your head. About $3,000 to $5,500 all-in.
  • Before paying anyone for design work, spend $100 to $200 and a weekend or two on the bench tests. They check whether the dish-gate nail actually feels like a scratch through your hair.

What an engineer adds

Worth paying for

  • A hands-on safety review of a device worn on your head, checked on real hardware. This is the one thing building it yourself can't supply. About $1,500–4,000 on its own.
  • Catching what this AI program can't see: parts as they actually print, sticky sliding surfaces, creaking cables, cracked joints, awkward handling. A good engineer finds this kind of problem in the first hour of review.
  • Real CAD and toleranced drawings, which every later quote and supplier depends on.
  • Reliability, so the prototype survives 50 sessions rather than 5.
  • A path to a product: automatic drift and the second pad in the next version (roughly $10,000–30,000 of design), then a manufacturable version, typically $50,000–250,000 or more over one to two years.
  • IP know-how and suppliers when parts stall or you need 500 of something.

Not worth paying for

  • Before the bench tests pass. Whether the nail feels like a good scratch through your hair costs about $30–45 and a weekend to learn. Spending $10,000 on design first could perfect something that fails on contact.
  • To make it feel better. No engineer moves the chance it feels good much above about 40 %; only testing on your head does.
  • To get there sooner, or for studio-grade process on a one-off you'll never sell.

A note if you might ever sell it: sharing the design publicly, for example in a build log online, starts the clock on patent rights. Keep it private until you decide, and have anyone you hire sign an IP assignment. A patent attorney consult typically costs a few hundred dollars.

What I need from you to narrow this down

  1. Your budget ceiling for this first build, and separately for a later version or product.
  2. Your target date for a first session, and whether six months is a hard line.
  3. Your experience with soldering, 3D printing, Linux and air fittings, and whether you enjoy building.
  4. Whether you own a 3D printer with a bed of at least 22 cm.
  5. Whether you'd ever want to sell it: yes, maybe or never.
  6. How far you'd drive to a builder, and whether a builder could work in your apartment.
  7. Whether you want an independent safety sign-off before the first session even if you build it yourself. I recommend yes.
