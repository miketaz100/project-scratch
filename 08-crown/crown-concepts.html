Project Scratch · SP1 mount decision

Crown Concepts

Three ways to rest the scratching hand on your head instead of hanging it from a desk arm. The hand itself is the same in all three: three nails at 24 mm pitch on spring leaves, one stroke servo on an 80 mm pendulum arm, a vertical float that lets a small dead weight set the force. Pick one; the rest of the package gets re-cut around it.

True of all three
  • Nail force is the floating dead weight, so the crown's seating slop does not change it. The chin strap only stops the crown sliding or twisting, and it must be elastic or magnetic so the whole thing comes off one-handed.
  • The hand module as currently engineered weighs 305 to 346 g, so every crown lands at 430 to 480 g on the head against a 500 g limit. A lightening pass on the palm (thinner shells, the Feetech servo) is needed whichever you pick.
  • Coverage is the crown and the upper back of the head. None reaches the nape, temples or sides. The back of the head works when you lean forward.
  • Servo noise now has a bone-conduction path through the pads. All three flag it; a soft isolator and the quieter Feetech servo are the mitigations.
  • Cost sits $120 to $155 below the desk stand, and each is roughly a weekend of extra build on top of the hand.
Concept A

Ring Crown

Hard-hat suspension, no shell

A thin aluminium hoop with a rear ratchet rests on four padded feet (forehead, two parietal, upper occiput). A carbon-tube portal clamped to the hoop holds the elbow axis 130 mm above the band, with pitch detents and a ±40 mm fore-aft slide. The hand floats inside on its dead weight; the hoop and strap never touch the force path.

Mass on head
429 g at the lightest weight, up to 489 g
Pads
4, about 3 kPa each
Stability margin
2.9× upright, 1.1× at 30° tilt (so a ±30° tilt rule)
Chin strap
1.5 N per side elastic, magnetic fuse; retention only
Coverage
crown ±64 mm along the arc, plus upper occiput
Cost and time
+$77 over the hand alone, about 10 h
Power-loss state
lifted: a 7 g micro electromagnet on the float drops a spring lift (+13 g)

Biggest risk. the hoop creeps on the head under servo reaction and drag at low friction; retired by a 20-minute drag and tilt test on a mannequin (pass: under 2 mm).

Its team would fall back to the desk stand if creep exceeds 2 mm, the servo is rated intrusive through bone conduction, the forehead pad is uncomfortable at 20 minutes, or the experiment needs the heaviest weight or the nape.

Concept B

Tripod Cap

The smallest thing that can hold the hand

Three carbon-tube legs with 45 mm soft pads form a 140 mm triangle around the scratch patch, with the servo cradle and idler bolted to a hub tube behind the elbow. Nothing else. Lift it and reseat it anywhere the scalp faces up, including the upper back of the head when you lean forward.

Mass on head
442 g bare, 478 g with the first weight; a 53 g lightening list brings it to about 395 g
Pads
3 × 45 mm, 3 to 4 kPa
Stability margin
0.6× with no strap (rear pad lifts), 1.5× with the strap
Chin strap
elastic Y-strap at 2.5 N per side, magnetic buckle; required
Coverage
crown ±40 mm, upper occiput leaning forward; reseat to re-aim
Cost and time
+$38 over the hand alone, about 6 h
Power-loss state
hand rests at its dead weight; a +45 g latch is the fallback if the safety gate insists on a lift

Biggest risk. the cap rocks or creeps under the stroke reaction, with the rear pad lifting; retired by a drag test with a laser pointer on the hub watching for drift.

Its team would fall back to the desk stand if the safety gate rejects the limp state and the latch pushes the mass past 500 g, servo whine through the skull is intrusive, or the pads cannot be made comfortable for 20 minutes.

Concept C

Twin-Rail Arch Halo

Coverage without reseating

Two printed curved channel rails sit either side of the head on forehead, occiput and temple pads. Carriage shoes ride inside the channels so the whole hand module slides front to back and locks at six detents, from 65 mm ahead of the vertex to 89 mm behind it. The rails are concentric with the skull, so the float stays normal to the scalp at every detent.

Mass on head
446 g at the lightest weight, up to 506 g (the heaviest setting uses a trim cord instead of a slug)
Pads
4: forehead 70×40, occiput 70×40, two temple 45×30; 2 to 3.4 kPa
Stability margin
1.3× at peak servo torque, with the strap
Chin strap
elastic magnetic, 2.5 N per side; required
Coverage
6 detents over a 154 mm front-to-back arc; occiput at full force leaning forward
Cost and time
+$27 over the hand alone; most of the cost is print time for the curved rails
Power-loss state
hand rests at its dead weight; manual lift; a park-bias elastic offered as an option

Biggest risk. mass sits right at the 500 g line, then halo slip at the rear detents and bone-conducted servo noise.

Its team would fall back to the desk stand if the experiment needs 1.5 N routinely and the halo comes in over 440 g, the safety gate rejects the limp state, servo hum through the forehead is intrusive, or your head profile shifts the engagement more than the ±6 mm the detents can trim.

Director's recommendation

Build A, the Ring Crown, and use a bought hard-hat ratchet suspension as the hoop instead of forming aluminium. It has the largest stability margin by a wide factor, which matters because a crown that creeps turns the constant force into a wandering one and defeats the reason for the float. It is also the only concept whose power-loss state lifts the nails, which the safety review asked for, and its four-point adjustable band is a comfort pattern proven on construction sites for eight-hour shifts.

B is the fallback if speed wins. It is the least to build and the easiest to re-aim, but its 1.5× margin leaves little room, and it stands up only with the strap on. If the first bench test shows the ring also creeps, B will not do better.

C only if region wander proves decisive. It gives the most coverage without reseating, but it is the heaviest, the printed curved rails are the riskiest parts in any of the three, and its margin is the slimmest.

Whichever you pick, the first thing built is the crown on a mannequin head with the servo running for 20 minutes, before the hand is finished. That test, not this page, confirms the choice.

What happens after you pick

  1. The hand gets a lightening pass and the Feetech STS3032 servo, with the stroke rate capped at 2.5 per second for inertia.
  2. The package is re-cut around the crown: parts list, prints, assembly, and a protocol whose first stage is the hand resting on your head with the motor off.
  3. Build, sensation and safety reviews run on the crown build. The desk stand stays in the repo as an appendix.
